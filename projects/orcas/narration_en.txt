## 1 — The paradox

Imagine you are floating in cold, dark water. Twenty metres away, a black fin rises. Then another. Then five more.
You are now sharing the sea with the most successful predator on the planet.
This animal hunts great white sharks and tears out their livers. It drowns blue whales, the largest animals that have ever lived. It makes waves to wash seals off the ice. It throws itself onto beaches to snatch sea lions from the shore.
Nothing in the ocean is safe from it.
Except, it seems, you.
In the wild, across all of recorded history, there is not a single confirmed case of an orca killing a human.
The ocean's deadliest predator refuses to treat us as prey. And the reason why may be one of the strangest stories in the animal kingdom.

## 2 — The perfect killer

To understand how strange that is, we first have to understand what an orca really is.
The orca, or killer whale, is not really a whale in the way we usually picture one. It is the largest member of the oceanic dolphin family.
It can grow to nearly ten metres long and weigh up to ten tonnes. Females can live an estimated eighty to ninety years. Males, fifty to sixty.
Orcas live in every ocean on Earth, from the polar ice to the tropics. They hunt in coordinated family groups, and they have no natural predators at all.
Scientists estimate that there are around fifty thousand orcas in the world's oceans. Fifty thousand of the most capable predators that have ever lived, sharing the sea with millions of swimmers, surfers, divers and fishermen every year.
Fish, squid, seals, sea lions, dolphins, sharks, even the great whales. If it lives in the sea, some orca, somewhere, has learned to kill it.
A human swimmer is smaller, slower and weaker than almost everything on that list. By any logic, we should be easy prey.
So before we try to answer the question, let's look at what orcas can do, when they decide something is food.

## 3 — What an orca can do

In the waters around the Antarctic Peninsula, scientists from the United States' National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration followed a group of orcas that hunt seals resting on floating ice.
First, they rise and look at the seal, heads above the surface. Then they back off, line up, and charge the floe together. Just before impact, they dive. The water they push forward keeps going, as a wave.
If the seal holds on, they do it again. And again. And here is the detail that surprised everyone: Weddell seals made up only around fifteen percent of the seals on the ice, yet fourteen of the fifteen kills recorded were Weddell seals.
They were not taking whatever they found. They were choosing.
In Argentine Patagonia, some orcas ride a wave straight onto the beach, grab a sea lion pup, and twist back into the sea. It is extremely dangerous. An orca that misjudges the wave can end up stuck on the sand, where its own weight could kill it.
A local ranger reported that only seven orcas there were known to hunt this way, and only five of them taught it to younger whales. Young orcas follow an experienced hunter, stay close to the beach, and practise without fully committing.
Off Western Australia, in 2019, researchers watched a dozen or so orcas chase down and kill a blue whale. Around fifty orcas then gathered to share it. By 2021, the same team had documented three blue whale kills in the area.
And off South Africa, two male orcas named Port and Starboard drove great white sharks away from some of their favourite bays. In 2023, researchers watched Starboard, alone, kill a young great white in less than two minutes.
Dead great whites washed up on the beaches, each missing the same organ: its liver, huge and rich in oil. On a single day in February 2023, at least seventeen sevengill sharks were found dead off the same coast, livers removed. In some places, shark cage diving, a whole local industry, came to a stop.
These are not random attacks. They are techniques. Planned, practised, and passed down.
An animal that can hunt a great white shark could hunt a human in seconds. So why doesn't it?

## 4 — The record

Let's look at the evidence. Every known encounter between wild orcas and people.
In September 1972, near Point Sur in California, a surfer named Hans Kretschmer said he was bitten by an orca. He needed around a hundred stitches. Experts think it was a case of mistaken identity. He was wearing a black wetsuit, and sea lions were nearby.
The orca bit, and then let go. It did not come back.
In 1989, in Patagonia, researcher Bernd Würsig was standing near the water when a beaching orca surged towards him. It missed him by about a metre. He ran up the beach, unhurt.
In August 2005, near Ketchikan in Alaska, a twelve-year-old boy named Ellis Miller was swimming in shallow water when a transient orca, one of the seal hunters, bumped him on the shoulder. Then it swam away. He was not bitten.
In 2014, in New Zealand, a freediver was dragged underwater for more than forty seconds by an orca. But the orca had grabbed a bag tied to his arm, not the man. An expert called it an accidental entanglement.
Over many decades, that is about all the evidence there is. A handful of bites, bumps and close calls.
The only death ever claimed is an old story from the Canadian Arctic, told by Inuit elders: a young man killed in the 1950s near orcas trapped in the ice. Researchers could never confirm it, and some think the cold water was the more likely cause.
Compare that with sharks. In 2025 alone, the International Shark Attack File recorded sixty-five unprovoked shark bites on people around the world, and nine deaths.
Orcas are bigger, smarter and better hunters than any shark. Some of them hunt sharks. And yet their record with us is almost empty.
But there is one place where that record looks very different.

## 5 — The dark exception

In captivity, the story changes.
There have been dozens of violent incidents between captive orcas and their trainers. And at least four people have died.
Three of those deaths involved a single male orca, Tilikum. The last was the SeaWorld trainer Dawn Brancheau, in 2010.
Researchers who study wild orcas point to what captivity takes away. In the wild, orcas swim dozens of kilometres every day with their families. In a tank, they have neither the space nor the social life they evolved for.
The contrast is striking. Free orcas, with every opportunity, almost never hurt us. Confined orcas, under stress, sometimes do.
So whatever protects us in the wild, it is not that orcas are incapable of harming humans. It is something else. Something they bring with them from the open ocean.
And the first clue lies in what orcas choose to eat.

## 6 — Theory one: we are not on the menu

In the 1970s, a Canadian scientist named Michael Bigg began studying the orcas off British Columbia, learning to recognise each whale by the shape of its dorsal fin.
He noticed something odd. In the very same waters, there were two kinds of orcas. And they did not mix.
The first kind lived in large, tight family groups and ate fish, almost always salmon. Today they are known as residents.
The second kind roamed in smaller groups and ignored the salmon entirely. They hunted seals, sea lions and other whales. Today they carry his name: Bigg's killer whales.
Side by side, they look almost identical. But genetic studies suggest the two lines split more than three hundred thousand years ago. In 2024, a team of researchers even proposed that they are two separate species. The scientific community has not fully agreed.
Here is what matters for us. Each orca population has its own menu, and it sticks to it. Whale researcher Erich Hoyt calls orcas fussy eaters. They eat what they learn to eat from their mothers and their families. Nothing more.
A fish-eating resident orca does not suddenly decide to try a seal. And a seal-hunting orca has no tradition that says a human is food.
To an orca, food is not everything that moves. Food is what your family has always eaten.
And that knowledge has to come from somewhere. In orca society, it comes from the oldest females.

## 7 — A twist: killers that don't eat

But wait. If orcas only harm what they eat, the story would be simple. It isn't.
The salmon-eating Southern Residents have a strange habit. They chase porpoises. They push them, toss them, sometimes for hours. And sometimes the porpoise dies.
In 2023, a study led by Deborah Giles, of the research group Wild Orca, and Sarah Teman, of the University of California, Davis, reviewed more than sixty years of records. They found seventy-eight documented cases of Southern Residents harassing porpoises between 1962 and 2020.
They never eat them. Porpoises are not on their menu.
The researchers proposed three explanations. It could be social play that helps the group bond. It could be practice, with a porpoise standing in for a fast salmon. Or it could be a kind of misplaced caregiving, as if the orcas were trying to look after a small, weak animal.
So orcas can harm animals they do not eat. Which makes their behaviour around people even more remarkable.
Humans swim, surf, dive and sail in orca waters every day. And still, no wild orca has ever been confirmed to have killed one of us.

## 8 — Theory two: the grandmothers

Very few animals go through menopause. Humans do. And, as far as we know, only five other species do: all of them toothed whales. Orcas are one of them.
A female orca stops having calves partway through her life. But she can live for decades afterwards.
Researchers from the University of Exeter found part of the answer by studying the resident orcas of the North Pacific over decades.
When salmon are scarce, older post-menopausal females are more likely to lead the group as it travels and searches for food. They seem to carry the knowledge of where the fish can be found, built up over a lifetime of good years and bad ones.
In a 2019 study of almost four hundred young orcas, calves whose grandmother had died in the previous two years had a mortality rate four and a half times higher than calves with a living grandmother.
In a society built on knowledge, the elders are the library. They teach where to hunt, how to hunt, and what to hunt.
And, perhaps, what to leave alone.
Because if hunting traditions can be passed down for generations, so can the absence of one. No grandmother has ever taught her family that humans are prey.

## 9 — Theory three: an intelligent, careful animal

There may be another reason. Orcas are not just strong. They are careful.
They have one of the largest brains in the ocean, and they live in complex societies, with family bonds that can last a lifetime.
On July twenty-fourth, 2018, off the coast of British Columbia, a female resident orca known as Tahlequah, or J35, gave birth to a calf. The calf died within about half an hour.
She did not let go. She carried the body for about seventeen days, over roughly one thousand six hundred kilometres. Other members of her pod were seen taking turns.
At the very end of 2024, she did it again. A new calf, a female, died within days, and Tahlequah was seen carrying her body into the new year.
We cannot know what an orca feels. But an animal that behaves like this is not a simple hunting machine. It reacts to its world with something we struggle to name.
Hoyt says wild orcas are generally careful around people underwater.
To an animal like this, a strange, slow, upright creature in the water may simply be something to investigate, not something to eat.

## 10 — Theory four: a promise

For thousands of years, people along the coast of the North Pacific have lived alongside orcas. And some of their stories offer an answer of their own.
One Tlingit story tells of Natsilane, a wood carver who was abandoned at sea by his own family. He carved a killer whale from wood, and it came to life and took revenge on those who betrayed him.
Afterwards, Natsilane regretted the killing. And he told the whale that it must never harm a human again.
It is a legend, not science. But it says something people noticed long before scientists did: the ocean's greatest hunter seems to have an unwritten rule about us.
And in the last few years, one group of orcas has put that rule to the test.

## 11 — The test case

Since 2020, sailors off the coasts of Spain and Portugal have reported a strange encounter. A group of orcas approaches a sailboat, goes straight for the rudder, and pushes, bites and spins it.
More than seven hundred interactions have been logged. At least eight boats have sunk.
Online, the story became a revenge tale: orcas fighting back against humans. Most scientists studying them do not support that idea. Young orcas seem to copy the behaviour from older ones, the way a game spreads through a playground.
But look at what they target. The rudder. Not the people.
Across hundreds of encounters, with crews standing on deck just a few metres away, no one has been injured by these orcas.
Even when orcas develop a brand-new habit that involves humans, they still leave the humans alone.

## 12 — The real danger

So the answer to our question is not one thing. It is a combination. We are not on the menu. The elders never taught it. And orcas are careful, curious animals that rarely mistake us for anything they eat.
But there is a darker side to this story. Because if anything in this relationship is dangerous, it is us.
The Southern Residents, a community of fish-eating orcas off Washington State and British Columbia, depend on Chinook salmon. And Chinook salmon have declined.
In 1995, there were ninety-eight Southern Residents. By the end of 2021, there were seventy-three. The 2026 census counted seventy-six, and in October 2026, a new calf was spotted, bringing the count to seventy-seven.
The Iberian orcas, the ones spinning rudders, number fewer than fifty animals, and are considered critically endangered.
The animals that refuse to hunt us are the ones that need our protection.

## 13 — Conclusion

We gave them a frightening name: killer whale.
And they are killers. Perhaps the most skilled hunters in the ocean. They can take down a great white shark, a blue whale, a seal on the ice.
But when they meet us, they look. They wonder. And they move on.
Not because they can't. But because, as far as we can tell, nothing in their culture tells them to.
