## 1 — Cold open

Antarctica. A seal rests on a slab of floating ice. It is out of the water. It should be safe.
Then the fins appear. Five, six, seven of them, lined up side by side.
They don't attack the ice. They swim at it, together, and dive at the last second. A wave rises behind them and rolls across the floe.
The seal slides. Once. Twice. The hunters turn and come again.
This is not instinct. It is a technique. It has to be learned, practised and passed on, from one generation to the next.
And it is only one of many hunting traditions, spread across the oceans of the world, all practised by a single animal.
What if the most dangerous predator in the sea is not just a hunter… but a culture?

## 2 — One species, many worlds

The orca, or killer whale, is not really a whale in the way we usually picture one. It is the largest member of the oceanic dolphin family.
It can grow to nearly ten metres long and weigh up to ten tonnes. Females can live an estimated eighty to ninety years. Males, fifty to sixty.
Orcas live in every ocean on Earth, from the polar ice to the tropics. And almost nothing in the sea is safe from them: fish, seals, sea lions, sharks, even other whales.
But here is the strange part. No single orca eats all of these things.
Each population has its own menu, its own hunting methods and its own social life. They share the same body, but they live in completely different worlds.
Scientists call these groups ecotypes. And to understand them, we have to start where orcas have been studied longer than anywhere else: the coast of the North Pacific.
Over the next twenty minutes, we will meet eight of these orca worlds. Some hunt with waves. Some hunt on land. One pair has driven the ocean's most famous shark from its own coastline.
By the end, you may never see the word "killer" in quite the same way again.

## 3 — The orcas that never mix

Chapter one: the orcas that never mix.
In the 1970s, a Canadian scientist named Michael Bigg began studying the orcas off British Columbia, learning to recognise each whale by the shape of its dorsal fin.
He noticed something odd. In the very same waters, there were two kinds of orcas. And they did not mix.
The first kind lived in large, tight family groups and ate fish, almost always salmon. Today they are known as residents.
The second kind roamed in smaller groups and ignored the salmon entirely. They hunted seals, sea lions and other whales. Today they carry his name: Bigg's killer whales.
Side by side, they look almost identical. But genetic studies suggest the two lines split more than three hundred thousand years ago.
In 2024, a team of researchers proposed that they are not one species at all, but two, with two separate scientific names: Orcinus ater for the residents, and Orcinus rectipinnus for Bigg's.
The scientific community has not fully agreed. That same year, the Society for Marine Mammalogy recognised them as two subspecies, but stopped short of calling them separate species.
Either way, the lesson is clear. To an orca, what you eat, how you hunt and who you travel with is not a detail. It defines who you are.
And nowhere is that more obvious than at the bottom of the world.

## 4 — The wave makers

Chapter two: the wave makers.
In the waters around the Antarctic Peninsula, scientists from the United States' National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration followed a group they called pack ice killer whales.
These orcas specialise in seals resting on floating ice. And they have solved a problem that should be impossible for an animal that cannot leave the water.
First, they find a seal. Several orcas rise and look at it, heads above the surface. Researchers call this spy-hopping.
Then they back off, line up, and charge the floe together, beating their tails. Just before impact, they dive. The water they push forward keeps going, as a wave.
If the seal holds on, they do it again. And again. The researchers saw groups repeat the attack for half an hour or more. Every wave costs energy. Every wave has to be timed together. This is coordination, not chance.
And here is the detail that surprised everyone. Among the seals resting on the ice, Weddell seals made up only around fifteen percent. Yet in the kills the team recorded, fourteen out of fifteen were Weddell seals.
The orcas were not taking whatever they found. They were choosing. Why they prefer Weddell seals is still unknown.
A wave as a weapon. Teamwork as a tool. But some orcas take an even bigger risk. They leave the ocean altogether.

## 5 — The beach raiders

Chapter three: the beach raiders.
Punta Norte, in Argentine Patagonia. Every year, sea lion colonies on these pebble beaches fill with pups.
In March and April, the youngest pups start to play in the shallows. They are clumsy, slow, and very close to the waves.
Out at sea, a tall black fin is waiting.
Then the orca does something no ocean predator should do. It rides a wave straight onto the beach, grabs a pup, and twists its body to slide back into the sea.
It is extremely dangerous. An orca that misjudges the wave can end up stuck on the sand, where its own weight could kill it.
This is why the technique has to be taught. A local ranger reported that only seven orcas here were known to hunt this way, and only five of them taught it to younger whales.
Young orcas have been seen following an experienced hunter, staying close to the beach, sharing the catch, practising without fully committing.
That same ranger called it a culture at risk. If the few teachers disappear, the knowledge disappears with them.
Small prey, big risk. But some orcas have gone in the opposite direction. They hunt the biggest animal that has ever lived.

## 6 — Hunting a giant

Chapter four: hunting a giant.
The blue whale. Around twenty metres long or more. The largest animal ever known to have existed. For a long time, scientists doubted that anything could kill a healthy adult.
In March 2019, off the south coast of Western Australia, researchers on a boat near a deep-sea canyon watched it happen.
A dozen or so orcas had found a blue whale. They had been chasing it for a long time.
The orcas bit into its body and flanks. Two of them pushed onto the whale, forcing it under, until it could no longer breathe.
Then, more orcas arrived. Around fifty gathered to share the carcass.
Strangely, the first thing they ate was the tongue, probably while the whale was still alive. Nobody knows why it is their preferred cut.
It was not a one-off. By 2021, the same research team had documented three separate blue whale kills in the area, published in the journal Marine Mammal Science.
One of the researchers said it raised the bar higher than he had ever imagined. And as blue whale numbers slowly recover from whaling, these hunts may become more common.
But the most dramatic orca story of the last decade is not about size. It is about two individuals. And a fish that every swimmer fears.

## 7 — The shark hunters

Chapter five: the shark hunters.
For decades, the coast of South Africa was famous for one thing: great white sharks. Tourists came from around the world to see them from cages.
Then, starting around 2015, dead great whites began washing up on the beaches. Each one had the same wound. And each one was missing the same organ: its liver.
The shark's liver is huge and rich in oil. For a predator, it is pure energy.
The prime suspects were two male orcas, easy to recognise because their tall dorsal fins had collapsed. One bends to the left, one to the right.
Like the sides of a ship, they were named Port and Starboard. They had first been reported in 2009, off the coast of Namibia, long before anyone connected them to the dead sharks.
At least seven dead great whites were found in 2017 alone. Within a few years, great white sightings in some of their favourite bays dropped to almost nothing.
The ocean's most famous predator had been pushed off its own coastline. In some places, shark cage diving, a whole local industry built around great whites, came to a stop.
Then, in June 2023, researchers saw something new. Starboard, alone, caught a young great white and killed it in less than two minutes. Then he swam past their boat carrying a piece of its liver. The next day, a second, larger great white washed up nearby, also without its liver, probably from an earlier attack nobody saw.
The pair have also killed other shark species. On a single day in February 2023, at least seventeen sevengill sharks were found dead off the same coast, livers removed.
Scientists still don't know why these two started hunting sharks, or whether others will learn from them. But in the world of orcas, a new habit can spread.
And the ones who guide what the group knows, it turns out, are not the biggest males.

## 8 — The grandmothers

Chapter six: the grandmothers.
Very few animals go through menopause. Humans do. And, as far as we know, only five other species do: all of them toothed whales. Orcas are one of them.
A female orca stops having calves partway through her life. But she can live for decades afterwards.
For a long time, that made no evolutionary sense. Why live so long if you can no longer reproduce?
Researchers from the University of Exeter found part of the answer by studying the resident orcas of the North Pacific over decades.
When salmon are scarce, older post-menopausal females are more likely to lead the group as it travels and searches for food. They seem to carry the knowledge of where the fish can be found, built up over a lifetime of good years and bad ones.
In a 2019 study of almost four hundred young orcas, calves whose grandmother had died in the previous two years had a mortality rate four and a half times higher than calves with a living grandmother.
And in 2024, a comparison of toothed whale species found that females in species with menopause live around forty years longer than females of similar species without it.
The extra years are not wasted. They are spent leading, protecting and teaching.
In a society built on knowledge, the elders are the library. And when a member of the family dies, these whales seem to feel it.

## 9 — A mother's grief

Chapter seven: a mother's grief.
On July twenty-fourth, 2018, off the coast of British Columbia, a female resident orca known as Tahlequah, or J35, gave birth to a calf.
The calf died within about half an hour.
But Tahlequah did not let go. She pushed the body ahead of her, balancing it on her head, and carried it as the family travelled.
Day after day. A week. Two weeks. Other members of her pod were seen taking turns carrying the calf.
She carried it for about seventeen days, over roughly one thousand six hundred kilometres. Then she let it go, and rejoined her family.
The world watched. And many people asked a question science is still careful with: was this grief?
We cannot know what an orca feels. But we can see what she does. And at the very end of 2024, she did it again. A new calf, a female, died within days, and Tahlequah was seen carrying her body into the new year.
Her story is also the story of her population. And that population is in trouble.

## 10 — A population in danger

Tahlequah belongs to the Southern Residents, a community of orcas that lives around the waters between Washington State and British Columbia.
They depend on one kind of fish above all: Chinook salmon. And Chinook salmon have declined.
In 1995, there were ninety-eight Southern Residents. By the end of 2021, there were seventy-three. They are listed as endangered in both the United States and Canada.
Their waters are also contaminated by pollutants that build up in the bodies of top predators.
There is some good news. The 2026 census counted seventy-six Southern Residents, the second year in a row with a small increase. And in October 2026, a new calf was spotted, bringing the count to seventy-seven. Researchers and governments have argued for years over how to help them, from removing dams to restoring salmon runs.
Seventy-seven whales. Small enough that every birth and every death changes the future of the whole community.
Meanwhile, on the other side of the Atlantic, another tiny orca population has made headlines for a very different reason.

## 11 — The rudder raiders

Chapter eight: the rudder raiders.
Since 2020, sailors off the coasts of Spain and Portugal have reported a strange encounter. A group of orcas approaches a sailboat, goes straight for the rudder, and pushes, bites and spins it.
More than seven hundred interactions have been logged. At least eight boats have sunk.
Online, the story became a revenge tale: orcas fighting back against humans. Most scientists studying them do not support that idea.
The Iberian orca population is tiny, fewer than fifty animals, and considered critically endangered. Only around fifteen of them have been involved in the incidents.
The leading explanation is social learning. Young orcas seem to copy the rudder behaviour from older ones, and the habit has spread through the group, the way a game spreads through a playground.
It may have started with one bad encounter with a boat. It may have no deeper meaning at all. But it shows, once again, how fast an idea can travel through an orca society.
And that brings us back to the question at the start of this film.

## 12 — What makes an orca

Wave makers in Antarctica. Beach hunters in Patagonia. Blue whale hunters in Australia. Shark hunters in South Africa. Salmon specialists in the North Pacific. Rudder spinners in Spain.
Physically, they are almost the same animal. What separates them is what they have learned.
Their hunting methods are passed from one generation to the next. Their food preferences are traditions. Their knowledge is carried by the oldest females.
That is what many scientists mean when they say orcas have culture. Not art, not language as we know it, but behaviour that is learned socially and shared within a group.
And culture can be fragile. When a population shrinks, it can lose more than individuals. It can lose a way of life, a technique, a map of where food can be found.
The Patagonian beach hunters depend on a handful of teachers. The Southern Residents depend on salmon, and on the rivers where salmon are born. The Iberian orcas number fewer than fifty. Each of these cultures hangs by a thread, and each thread is different.
Protecting orcas is not just about saving a species. It is about saving many different ways of being an orca.

## 13 — Conclusion

We gave them a frightening name: killer whale.
But watch them long enough and a different picture appears. Families that travel together. Grandmothers who lead. Mothers who will not let go.
Hunters, yes. Perhaps the most skilled hunters in the ocean. But also teachers, students and keepers of traditions passed down for generations.
Out there, right now, a young orca is watching its elders, learning how its family has always survived.
The question is whether we will leave enough ocean for those lessons to be passed on.
