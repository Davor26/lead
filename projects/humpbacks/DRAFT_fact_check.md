## Core facts (well established)
- **Pitman et al. (2017), *Marine Mammal Science*:** "Humpback whales interfering when mammal-eating killer whales attack other species: Mobbing behavior and interspecific altruism?" They compiled 115 humpback–killer whale interactions from reports going back to the 1950s.
  - Humpbacks usually approached the orcas.
  - The orcas were almost always mammal-eaters (Bigg's/transients, or Antarctic types).
  - Where the prey was identified, it was another species about 89% of the time.
  - The proposed explanation: humpbacks respond to the sound of an orca attack whatever the prey, because ignoring an attack on their own calves costs too much.
- **Antarctic Peninsula, 2009.** Pitman and Durban watched orcas wash a Weddell seal off a floe. The seal ended up on the chest of a humpback lying on its back, which nudged it back with a flipper when it slipped. The seal then reached another floe.
- **Monterey Bay, May 2012.** Bigg's orcas killed a gray whale calf. Humpbacks arrived and stayed near the carcass for hours, trumpeting and tail-slapping, and disrupted the orcas' feeding. Observer: Alisa Schulman-Janiger.
- **Humpback biology:**
  - about 14–16 m long and roughly 30+ t;
  - flippers up to about 5 m, nearly a third of body length; *Megaptera* means "big wing";
  - barnacles on the flippers;
  - individual fluke patterns used for photo-ID;
  - only males sing.
- **Orca rake scars:** frequent on humpback flukes, mostly picked up as calves.
- **Whaling:** humpbacks were protected worldwide in 1966. The IUCN lists them as Least Concern (since 2008).

## Please verify (wording I chose or rounded)
1. "**Heavier than five adult orcas put together**" (Ch. 2): 30 t humpback against orcas of about 4–6 t. This is a rough comparison.
2. "**About nine cases out of ten**" were another species, and "**at least ten different prey species**" (Ch. 8). The list in the script includes an ocean sunfish. Please check these against the paper's table.
3. "**Up to about a third of adult humpbacks**" carry rake marks "in some populations" (Ch. 9). Published rates vary from a few percent to about 40% by region.
4. Monterey 2012: "**first a pair, then more**" and "**more than six hours**". Reports say about 6.5–7 hours; please check the number of humpbacks if you want to quote one.
5. **Pitman's "compassion" remark** (Ch. 12) is paraphrased, not quoted. The text card says "quote paraphrased".
6. "**Since the 2017 study, new reports have kept coming**" (Ch. 13). This is general; I didn't cite a specific new paper.
7. "**Only a few percent of the original population survived**" in some regions (Ch. 13). This holds for some stocks, for example parts of the Southern Hemisphere.
