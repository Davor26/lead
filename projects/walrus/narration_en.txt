## 1 — The paradox

A polar bear stands on a ridge of ice above the shore. Below it, a walrus lies asleep on the stones.
The bear lifts a heavy block of ice in its front paws.
It takes aim, and throws it at the walrus's head. Then it waits to see whether the giant below will move again.
For more than a century, Inuit hunters in Greenland and the Canadian Arctic have described scenes like this one. Hunters who knew the ice better than anyone said they had seen it with their own eyes.
And for just as long, many scientists treated those stories as legends. Nobody had filmed it, and nobody had measured it.
Because it makes no sense.
The polar bear is the largest predator that lives on land. It can kill a seal with a single blow. Its paws are as wide as dinner plates, and its sense of smell can find a seal under a metre of snow.
So why would the most powerful hunter in the Arctic ever need a weapon?
The answer is lying on that beach. And it weighs more than a tonne.

## 2 — A fortress with tusks

To understand the problem, look at the two animals side by side. On paper, the bear should win every time.
A large male polar bear can weigh around six hundred kilograms. A large male walrus can weigh more than twice as much.
A walrus is not a seal. It is closer to a fortress. Even its body is built for conflict.
Its skin can be several centimetres thick on the neck and shoulders. Under the skin lies a deep layer of blubber, and under that, heavy muscle.
Claws and teeth that open a seal in seconds barely get through. The thickest skin covers exactly the places where rival males strike each other with their tusks during the breeding season.
And then there are the tusks. They are canine teeth that never stop growing, and in a big male they can approach a metre in length.
A walrus uses them to haul itself out of the water, to fight its rivals, and to defend itself.
For a bear, attacking an adult walrus head on is not hunting. It is a gamble with its own life.

## 3 — Life on the ice

Pacific walruses spend much of the year on the drifting sea ice of the Bering and Chukchi seas. They follow the ice as it grows in winter and shrinks in summer.
The ice is their raft. They rest on it between dives, give birth on it, and ride it north as it melts each summer.
Below the ice lies one of the richest seafloors on Earth: a shallow continental shelf covered with clams, worms and snails.
There, walruses gather by the thousands, pressed together, tusks resting on each other's backs. On a single floe, hundreds of animals can lie side by side, steaming in the cold air.
For a walrus, a crowd means safety. Every animal is another pair of eyes, and another pair of tusks.
For a polar bear, a crowd is a wall. And yet, every year, some bears try to break through it.
Most of the time, polar bears hunt seals, waiting beside a breathing hole in the ice for hours until one comes up for air. Walruses are something else entirely: bigger, rarer, and far more dangerous.

## 4 — Svalbard, 2015

In July 2015, on a beach in Svalbard, a photographer filmed a hungry polar bear and her cub approaching a herd of sleeping walruses.
The bear did not charge. She walked slowly among them, sniffing one walrus after another. Most of the walruses barely moved.
Scientists who studied the footage suggested she was testing them, looking for one that was weak, sick, or already dead. A healthy adult walrus was simply too dangerous to attack.
Then she touched one with her paw.
The walrus woke up. And it was the bear that backed away. A predator weighing hundreds of kilograms had retreated from an animal that had only lifted its head.
Without a meal, experts feared that the cub would not survive for long.

## 5 — Wrangel Island, 1990

To see what really happens between the two species, scientists went to Wrangel Island, in the Russian Arctic. The island is a nature reserve, sometimes called the maternity ward of the polar bear, because so many females dig their dens there.
In the autumn of 1990, the Russian biologists Nikita Ovsyanikov and Anatoly Kochnev watched the island's walrus haul-outs, day after day.
Thousands of walruses crowded onto the beaches. And the bears came too, sometimes dozens of them. When the sea ice drifted away in autumn, many bears were left on the island with little else to eat.
The bears did not attack the herd from the front. They walked along its edge, watching. Some lay down near the walruses for hours, as if they were waiting for a signal.
They were waiting for one thing: the moment the herd would panic.

## 6 — When the walrus fights back

When a bear comes too close, an adult walrus does not run. It turns to face it. Its size, which makes it slow on land, suddenly becomes its greatest strength.
It raises its head, opens its mouth, and swings its tusks.
A single strike can drive a tusk deep into a bear's neck or shoulder. And unlike a bear, the walrus does not need to win the fight. It only needs to make the attacker give up.
Polar bears have been seriously injured, and even killed, by walruses defending themselves.
On Wrangel Island, the observers counted the attacks. Every approach, every charge, every retreat.
Eleven of them were attacks on adult walruses. Bears charged, circled and tested them, sometimes again and again.
Not one of those adults was killed.
The bears seemed to know it. Most of them avoided adult walruses altogether, and turned their attention to something smaller.

## 7 — The stampede

So the bears use another strategy. They do not attack the walrus. They attack the crowd.
A bear runs toward the herd. Panic spreads, and thousands of animals rush for the water at the same time. Walruses that weigh more than a tonne climb over each other to reach the sea.
In the chaos, the smallest animals are knocked over, pinned and crushed. Young walruses, separated from their mothers, are caught under animals many times their weight.
That is when the bears move in. Not on the giants, but on the calves.
In one season of observations, the scientists recorded about twenty-five hunting attempts. Only two ended in a kill. Both were calves.
Far more walruses died in the stampedes themselves.
Of the walrus carcasses examined on the island's beaches, around forty-three percent were calves.
For the bear, the stampede is a weapon of its own. But it is not the only one.

## 8 — The weapon

But some stories describe something stranger.
In 2021, three of the most experienced polar bear scientists in the world, Ian Stirling, Kristin Laidre and Erik Born, published a study in the journal Arctic.
They gathered Inuit accounts and historical reports from Greenland and the Canadian Arctic, going back more than a century. Some came from hunters, some from explorers, some from naturalists who had spent their lives on the ice.
Again and again, the stories described the same scene: a bear picking up a block of ice or a stone, and throwing it at the head of a walrus.
The target is always the same. The head. It is where a stunned walrus can no longer fight back. Elsewhere, the walrus is protected by skin, blubber and muscle. The skull is the one place where a heavy blow can do real damage.
The scientists also pointed to a young male polar bear named GoGo, at a zoo in Osaka, Japan.
Faced with food hung out of his reach, GoGo threw sticks and other objects at it until it fell.
Using a tool is rare in the animal world. Chimpanzees fish for termites with sticks, sea otters crack shells on stones, and some crows bend twigs into hooks. Among large predators, it is almost unheard of.
The authors concluded that tool use by wild polar bears is probably rare. But it is likely real, and it seems to appear in one situation above all: when the prey is a walrus.
Not every scientist agrees. Some argue that stories, however many, are not enough evidence.
But if the accounts are right, the polar bear is one of very few mammals that uses a tool to hunt.

## 9 — Whiskers and clams

To understand why a walrus is worth that risk, follow it to the bottom of the sea.
A walrus feeds in the dark, in mud and silt, often dozens of metres down. It can stay underwater for up to half an hour, searching the bottom with its face.
It finds its food by touch. Its snout carries hundreds of thick, sensitive whiskers, able to feel the shape of a clam buried in the mud.
Then it uses its tongue like a piston. It pulls it back sharply, creating a powerful suction that pulls the clam right out of its shell.
A walrus can also blow jets of water into the seafloor to uncover its prey.
In a single feeding trip, one walrus can eat three thousand to six thousand clams. It does not crush the shells. It leaves them almost untouched on the seafloor, emptied.
All that food becomes blubber. And blubber is exactly what a polar bear needs.

## 10 — Mothers

A walrus calf is born on the ice in spring, and it stays with its mother for about two years. It drinks her milk for more than a year, long after it has learned to swim.
When danger comes, the mother dives with her calf, holds it against her body, or carries it on her back in the water.
Walrus mothers have been seen placing themselves between their calf and a bear. Other females in the herd sometimes help, forming a living barrier of bodies and tusks.
And that is why a stampede is so dangerous. In the panic, a mother can lose her calf, and the calf can be trampled by its own herd.

## 11 — Songs under the ice

Walruses are not only fighters. They are also surprisingly musical. Much of their life happens in sound.
In winter, males sing underwater for hours, with knocks, taps and long, bell-like tones. The sound can carry far through the cold water.
Each male's song is his own, repeated again and again to attract females and to warn rivals.
On land, walruses snort, roar and grumble. A mother and her calf can recognise each other's voices.
It is a society built on sound and touch, packed tightly together on the ice.

## 12 — The lost ice

For thousands of years, this system worked.
In summer, the ice edge retreated north, but it stayed above the shallow shelf, where walruses could rest on the ice and dive straight down to their food.
Now, in many summers, the ice melts back past the edge of the shelf, over water that is thousands of metres deep.
A walrus cannot feed that deep. It has to choose: stay on the ice and go hungry, or swim to the coast. Every trip between the coast and the feeding grounds costs energy, and calves pay the highest price.
Since 2007, the summer ice has retreated beyond the shelf in most years. What was once rare has become normal.
And so tens of thousands of walruses now come ashore in Alaska and Russia, often far from where they feed.

## 13 — The crowded beaches

In September 2014, near the village of Point Lay, Alaska, about thirty-five thousand walruses crowded onto a single beach.
Crowds like this are exactly what the bears of Wrangel Island learned to exploit.
In 1991, a single stampede on Wrangel Island left one hundred and four walruses trampled to death. Most of them were young.
Today, a stampede can be started by a bear, by a low-flying aircraft, by a boat, or even by a person walking on the beach.
In 2009, at Icy Cape in Alaska, scientists found more than one hundred young walruses crushed after a single stampede.
Villages near the haul-outs now ask pilots to fly high, and visitors to keep their distance. Some communities even close their beaches during the haul-out season.
And the bears are still there. Pushed ashore by the same melting ice, they walk the beaches, hungry.
Some of them may still pick up a block of ice.

## 14 — Conclusion

Today there are an estimated two hundred and fifty thousand Pacific walruses.
The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.
Scientists now count walruses from the air with drones and aircraft, and follow them with satellite tags, to understand how they are adapting to a changing sea.
Their future depends on the sea ice, and on how much of it is left over the shallow seas where they feed. The polar bear depends on that ice too. Without it, both animals are pushed onto the same narrow strip of land.
We often imagine the Arctic as a place of simple strength, where the biggest animal always wins.
But the walrus shows us a different picture.
Here, the largest predator on land sometimes needs patience, a crowd in panic, or a block of ice. It needs a plan.
Not because the bear is weak. But because the walrus is that strong.
