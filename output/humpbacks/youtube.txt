Why Humpback Whales Save Other Animals From Orcas

DESCRIPTION
Antarctica, 2009. Killer whales wash a seal off its ice floe. Then a humpback whale rolls onto its back, lifts the seal onto its chest, and holds it out of the water until the orcas give up.

A humpback does not eat seals. So why would a 30-tonne whale put itself between a pack of killer whales and their prey?

In this documentary, we follow the humpbacks that break up orca hunts: the Weddell seal rescue off the Antarctic Peninsula, the humpbacks that stood guard for hours in Monterey Bay in 2012, and the 2017 study by Robert Pitman and colleagues that collected 115 encounters between humpbacks and killer whales. In about nine cases out of ten, the animal being saved was not a humpback at all: seals, sea lions, gray whale calves, porpoises, even an ocean sunfish.

We look at the humpback's weapons (five-metre flippers, a crushing tail and very loud voices), the orca scars many of them carry from their first migration, and the big question: is this protection of their own kind, a simple rule, or something close to compassion?
